Executive Summary

This analysis examines the Electoral College system through constitutional law and political data lenses, analyzing the tension between Federalist No. 68’s original design rationale and modern electoral realities, evaluating the Supreme Court’s recent jurisprudence on faithless electors, quantifying voting power disparities across state populations, and providing a technical legal assessment of reform mechanisms like the National Popular Vote Interstate Compact.

I. Federalist No. 68: The Intermediary Body Justification

Original Constitutional Design Rationale

In Federalist No. 68 (1788), Alexander Hamilton articulated the framers’ rationale for an intermediary electoral body, emphasizing several critical design features:

“The most plausible of these [critics], who has appeared in print, has even deigned to admit that the election of the President is pretty well guarded. I venture somewhat further, and hesitate not to affirm, that if the manner of it be not perfect, it is at least excellent.”

Hamilton’s justification centered on four key principles:

Design Principle Hamilton’s Rationale Constitutional Purpose
Deliberative Selection “Immediate election should be made by men most capable of analyzing the qualities adapted to the station” Filter popular passion through informed judgment
Independence from Corruption “Executive should be independent for his continuance in office on all but the people themselves” Prevent executive dependency on Congress or foreign powers
Moral Certainty “Process of election affords a moral certainty that there will be a constant probability of seeing the station filled by characters preeminent for ability and virtue” Ensure quality of leadership through structured selection
Limited Scope “Special body of representatives, deputed by the society for the single purpose of making the important choice” Prevent permanent political power accumulation in electors

The Intermediary Body Concept

Hamilton envisioned electors as a temporary, ad-hoc deliberative assembly rather than a standing institution. His language emphasizes:

  • Temporal Limitation: Electors assemble “for the single purpose of making the important choice” and disperse
  • Information Superiority: A “small number of persons… most likely to possess the information and discernment requisite to such complicated investigations”
  • Judicial Independence: Insulation from immediate popular pressures while remaining ultimately accountable to voters

Theoretical Tension with Modern Reality

The fundamental tension emerges between Hamilton’s vision of a deliberative intermediary body and the contemporary reality where electors function as:

  1. Rubber Stamp Mechanisms (not independent deliberators)
  2. Partisan Agents (selected for loyalty rather than judgment)
  3. Fixed-State Representatives (bound by state law rather than constitutional discretion)

II. Modern Reality: Winner-Take-All Allocation System

Current State of the Electoral College

As of 2024, 48 states and the District of Columbia employ winner-take-all allocation, awarding all electoral votes to the statewide popular vote winner. Only Maine (since 1972) and Nebraska (since 1996) utilize district-based allocation:

Allocation Type States Electoral Vote Count
Winner-Take-All 48 states + DC 533 electoral votes
District-Based Maine, Nebraska 5 electoral votes combined

Mathematical Consequences of Winner-Take-All

The winner-take-all system creates disproportionate influence for:

  1. Swing States: Approximately 10-12 competitive states receive the majority of campaign attention and resources
  2. Safe States: Both overwhelmingly Democratic and Republican states experience reduced voter engagement
  3. Small States: Benefit from structural advantage in electoral vote allocation (discussed below)

Constitutional Basis for State Control

The Supreme Court has affirmed state authority over elector selection methodology:

  • McPherson v. Blake, 146 U.S. 1 (1892): “The appointment of electors and the mode of appointing them are left to the states, except as it is provided by the Constitution”
  • Bush v. Gore, 531 U.S. 98 (2000): Reaffirmed state legislative primacy in electoral administration

III. Chiafalo v. Washington (2020): Faithless Elector Jurisprudence

Case Background and Holding

In Chiafalo v. Washington, 591 U.S. ___ (2020), the Supreme Court unanimously upheld state laws binding electors to their pledged presidential candidates:

Key Holdings: - States possess authority to enforce elector pledges through penalties and replacement mechanisms - The Electoral College system does not guarantee independent judgment by individual electors - State legislative power under Article II, Section 1 encompasses election administration rules

Legal Reasoning Framework

Justice Ginsburg’s opinion established three critical principles:

Principle Constitutional Basis Implication
Plenary Power Doctrine Article II, Section 1 grants states authority over elector appointment States may regulate elector behavior
Historical Practice Electors have functioned as party loyalists since early republic Independence was never an operational reality
Federalism Protection State election administration falls within traditional police powers Federal non-interference presumption applies

Specific Legal Implications

1. Enforcement Authority

  • States may impose monetary penalties on faithless electors
  • States may replace faithless electors with alternate candidates
  • Penalties do not violate the Constitution’s elector selection framework

2. Constitutional Boundaries

The Court identified limits to state power: - Cannot prevent voters from expressing preferences in primaries - Cannot interfere with federal constitutional requirements (e.g., minimum electoral vote counts) - Must maintain due process protections for electors

3. Practical Consequences

  • Faithless elector incidents have become legally insignificant in most jurisdictions
  • The system now functions as a formalized party delegate mechanism rather than independent deliberative body
  • Reduced risk of constitutional crisis from individual elector defiance

Banzhaf Power Index Analysis

The Banzhaf voting power index measures each state’s probability of being a pivotal voter in close elections:

  • Small States: Higher per-vote influence due to fixed electoral vote minimum (3 electors)
  • Large States: Diluted influence as population grows without proportional electoral increase
  • Swing States: Enhanced practical importance regardless of population size

Game-Theoretic Implications

Candidate Resource Allocation Incentives

The “Portfolio Effect”

Candidates must construct an electoral map requiring: - Securing competitive state victories (swing states) - Maintaining base strength in safe states - Avoiding catastrophic losses in key regions

This creates a risk-minimization strategy that differs fundamentally from vote-maximization under pure popular vote systems.

V. National Popular Vote Interstate Compact: Legal Hurdles Analysis

Current Status and Mechanism

As of 2024, 19 jurisdictions have enacted the NPVIC legislation, possessing 222 electoral votes:

Category Jurisdictions Electoral Votes
Small States (6) Delaware, Hawaii, Maine, Rhode Island, Vermont, DC 13
Medium States (10) Colorado, Connecticut, Maryland, Massachusetts, Minnesota, New Jersey, New Mexico, Oregon, Virginia, Washington 129
Large States (3) California, Illinois, New York 80

Threshold Requirement: The Compact activates when member states possess 270 electoral votes (majority of 538 total).

Compact Clause Constitutional Framework

Article I, Section 10, Clause 3 Text

“No State shall, without the Consent of Congress… enter into any Agreement or Compact with another State”

Supreme Court Precedent on Interstate Compacts

Case Year Standard Established
Virginia v. Tennessee 1893 “Agreement” and “compact” are synonyms; congressional consent required only for compacts increasing state political power at federal expense
U.S. Steel Corp. v. Multistate Tax Commission 1978 Functional interpretation: congressional consent required when compact undermines federal supremacy
New Hampshire v. Maine 1976 Congressional approval necessary for compacts altering vertical power balance
Cuyler v. Adams 1981 Congress maintains supervisory authority over cooperative state action affecting national interests

Primary Constitutional Challenges

Challenge 1: Compact Clause Consent Requirement

CRS Report Analysis (2019) identifies three conditions for interstate compact status requiring congressional consent:

Condition NPVIC Compliance Legal Significance
Written Agreement Yes (formal compact text) Satisfies basic definition of compact
Reciprocal Obligations Yes (mutual binding commitments) Meets functional test for compact status
No Unilateral Modification Yes (requires unanimous state consent to exit) Confirms contractual nature under Virginia v. Tennessee

Challenge 2: Vertical Power Balance Shift

The most significant constitutional hurdle involves whether the NPVIC alters federal-state power dynamics:

Argument Against Congressional Consent Requirement:

  • Yale Law Professor Akhil Amar argues NPVIC does not create “new interstate governmental apparatus”
  • States exercise existing Article II powers individually, merely coordinating timing
  • No new sovereign entity created; states retain individual sovereignty

Counter-Argument Requiring Congressional Consent:

  • CRS report cites Northeast Bancorp precedent: any agreement covering all stipulations affecting party conduct constitutes compact
  • NPVIC removes contingent election possibility under 12th and 20th Amendments
  • Potential shift in federal-state power balance regarding presidential selection

Challenge 3: Plenary Power vs. Compact Coordination

Legal Question: Does coordinated state action constitute impermissible interstate compact or permissible parallel exercise of independent authority?

Position Legal Basis Support Level
Constitutional Without Consent States exercising individual Article II powers in coordination Supported by Amar and NPVIC proponents
Requires Congressional Consent Coordinated action alters federal election framework; constitutes interstate compact Supported by CRS analysis and traditional Compact Clause interpretation

Additional Legal Hurdles

1. Contingent Election Displacement

The NPVIC potentially eliminates the House of Representatives’ contingent election role under: - 12th Amendment: Requires contingent election if no candidate receives majority electoral votes - 20th Amendment: Specifies timing and procedures for presidential transitions

Legal Risk: Congressional consent may be required to alter established constitutional transition mechanisms.

2. Supremacy Clause Interaction

Once enacted, NPVIC states would operate under dual obligations: - State compact law (binding electors to national popular vote winner) - Potential federal election administration requirements

Supremacy Clause Concern: Conflicting state and federal authorities could trigger constitutional litigation.

3. Equal Protection and Voting Rights Act Considerations

While NPVIC proponents argue the Compact promotes equal protection through universal suffrage, opponents suggest: - State-specific implementation variations could create unequal voting conditions - Potential VRA challenges regarding minority vote dilution in district-based allocation states

VI. Synthesis and Conclusions

Constitutional Tension Summary

The Electoral College system embodies enduring tensions between:

  1. Federalist Design vs. Modern Reality: Hamilton’s deliberative intermediary body exists formally but functions as partisan delegate mechanism
  2. State Sovereignty vs. National Uniformity: States retain constitutional authority over elector selection while national popular vote advocates seek uniform standards
  3. Minority Protection vs. Majority Rule: Small state advantage protects regional interests at expense of proportional representation

Key Findings

Issue Constitutional Status Practical Reality
Electors as Intermediaries Constitutionally independent deliberators Function as party loyalists bound by state law
Winner-Take-All System State legislative discretion (McPherson precedent) 48 states + DC utilize this allocation method
Faithless Elector Penalties Constitutional under Chiafalo ruling (2020) Enforcement mechanisms active in compact states
Voting Power Disparity Constitutionally embedded structural feature Systematic advantage for small state voters (~274% vs. California)
NPVIC Compact Status Uncertain; CRS indicates congressional consent likely required 19 jurisdictions enacted; awaiting additional state ratifications

Technical Assessment of NPVIC Viability

The National Popular Vote Interstate Compact faces substantial but not insurmountable legal hurdles:

Most Likely Path to Activation:

  • Additional states enacting compact legislation (48 electoral votes needed)
  • Congressional consent secured through formal resolution or implied approval
  • Supreme Court validation if challenged on Compact Clause grounds

Alternative Constitutional Path:

  • Federal statutory reform eliminating winner-take-all provisions
  • Constitutional amendment establishing national popular vote system
  • State-by-state legislative adoption without compact mechanism

Final Observations

The Electoral College remains one of the most contested features of American constitutional design, reflecting fundamental disagreements about: - The proper role of state sovereignty in federal elections - The balance between regional representation and proportional democracy - The function of intermediary institutions in democratic governance

While Chiafalo v. Washington resolved immediate questions regarding elector independence, broader structural questions about voting power equity and compact mechanism constitutionality remain unresolved, awaiting either additional judicial clarification or political resolution through legislative action.
